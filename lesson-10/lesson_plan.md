## 2. Reading Section (35 min)

Two passages, 10 questions each (20 total). Show both passages on screen. Student reads and answers. Teacher starts the 35-minute timer.

### Passage 1: AI and the Future of Scientific Research

Artificial intelligence is transforming the landscape of scientific research at an extraordinary rate. Machine learning algorithms, which can process vast quantities of data far beyond human capability, are enabling breakthroughs in fields as diverse as medicine, climatology, and particle physics.

One of the most significant applications has been in drug discovery. Traditionally, identifying a single viable drug candidate could take over a decade and cost billions of dollars. AI systems can now screen millions of compounds in a matter of days, predicting which ones are most likely to be effective and safe. For example, an AI model developed at MIT identified a powerful new antibiotic — halicin — by analyzing over one hundred million molecular structures.

In climate science, AI is helping researchers build more accurate models of weather patterns and climate change. Deep learning systems can analyze satellite imagery and sensor data to predict extreme weather events with greater precision than ever before. These predictions enable earlier warnings and more effective disaster preparedness.

However, AI in science is not without limitations. Critics point out that AI models are only as good as the data they are trained on and that biased or incomplete data can lead to flawed conclusions. Moreover, the "black box" nature of many AI systems makes it difficult for researchers to understand exactly how a model arrived at its prediction — a significant problem in fields where understanding mechanism is as important as predicting outcome.

Despite these challenges, the trajectory is clear. AI will not replace scientists, but scientists who use AI will replace those who do not. The future of discovery lies not in human versus machine, but in human-machine collaboration.

#### Passage 1 Answer Key
1. (Vocabulary) "viable" → **feasible / workable**
2. (Detail) Limitation of AI → **AI models can produce flawed conclusions from biased data**
3. (Negative Fact) All mentioned EXCEPT → **sociology**
4. (Inference) Scientists who don't use AI → **They will be at a competitive disadvantage**
5. (Purpose) MIT halicin example → **To illustrate AI's impact on drug discovery**
6. (Sentence Insertion) "This represents..." → **After "screening millions of compounds" sentence**
7. (Pronoun Reference) "These predictions" → **Predictions of extreme weather events**
8. (Paraphrase) Final paragraph → **Scientists who incorporate AI will outperform those who do not**
9. (Attitude) Author's attitude → **Cautiously optimistic**
10. (Summary) Three main ideas → **A: AI accelerates drug discovery / C: AI improves climate modeling / D: AI has limitations but enables collaboration**

### Passage 2: Urban Development and Social Capital

Urban planners have long recognized that the physical design of a city profoundly influences the social relationships of its inhabitants. The concept of social capital — the networks, norms, and trust that enable cooperative behavior — is increasingly central to discussions about how cities should be built and redesigned. When neighborhoods are designed to encourage casual interaction, residents develop stronger community bonds, which in turn lead to lower crime rates and improved civic engagement.

Consider the difference between a traditional neighborhood with front porches, sidewalks, and mixed-use zoning, and a modern suburban development characterized by cul-de-sacs, large setbacks, and single-use zoning. In the traditional neighborhood, residents naturally encounter one another while walking to local shops or sitting on their porches. These seemingly trivial interactions — a wave, a brief conversation, a shared observation about the weather — accumulate over time into a fabric of mutual recognition and trust.

Research by sociologist Robert Putnam demonstrated that each additional ten minutes of commuting time reduces social capital by the equivalent of ten percent of all possible civic participation. This finding underscores a critical insight: the structure of a city determines not only how people move through space but also how they connect with one another. When commuting consumes a significant portion of daily life, there is simply less time and energy left for community involvement.

However, simply building sidewalks and parks does not guarantee social interaction. Urban sociologists emphasize that the design must also address safety, accessibility, and the presence of what urbanist Jane Jacobs called "eyes on the street" — natural surveillance created by people living, working, and gathering in the same area. A park that feels unsafe or is located far from residential areas will remain empty regardless of how well it is designed.

The lessons for contemporary urban planning are clear. Zoning regulations that separate residential areas from commercial districts should be reconsidered. Investment in public transit reduces commute times and frees time for community engagement. Mixed-use developments that combine housing, retail, and public spaces create the conditions for social capital to flourish. The goal is not merely to build efficient cities but to build connected communities.

#### Passage 2 Answer Key
1. (Vocabulary) "profoundly" → **deeply / significantly**
2. (Detail) What is social capital? → **Networks, norms, and trust that enable cooperative behavior**
3. (Negative Fact) All mentioned as factors encouraging interaction EXCEPT → **high-rise apartment buildings**
4. (Inference) What can be inferred about cul-de-sac neighborhoods? → **They may reduce casual encounters between neighbors**
5. (Purpose) Why mention Robert Putnam's research? → **To show the link between commuting time and civic participation**
6. (Sentence Insertion) "This was a dramatic shift from traditional urban design." → **After "cul-de-sacs, large setbacks, and single-use zoning"**
7. (Pronoun Reference) "This finding" → **The correlation between commute time and reduced social capital**
8. (Paraphrase) Jane Jacobs' "eyes on the street" → **Natural surveillance from people present in an area**
9. (Attitude) Author's attitude toward mixed-use development → **Strongly supportive**
10. (Summary) Three main ideas → **A: Urban design influences social capital / B: Commute time affects community involvement / D: Zoning reform and mixed-use development strengthen communities**

### Lecture 1: Digital Platforms and Economic Opportunity
**Audio file:** `audio/listening-lecture-1.wav`

> "Today we'll examine how digital platforms are reshaping economic opportunity in both developed and developing economies.
>
> First, let's consider the positive impact. Digital platforms like Etsy, Shopify, and Upwork have dramatically lowered barriers to entrepreneurship. In the past, starting a business required significant capital — you needed a physical storefront, inventory, and employees. Today, an artisan in rural Kenya can sell handmade goods to customers in New York without leaving their home. This democratization of commerce has created economic opportunities that simply didn't exist twenty years ago.
>
> However, the picture is more complex than it first appears. While platforms lower entry barriers, they also capture a significant portion of the value created. Most platforms take fifteen to thirty percent commission on each transaction — a rate that critics argue is excessive. Furthermore, the algorithms that determine visibility on these platforms are often opaque, meaning sellers have little control over who sees their products.
>
> In developing economies specifically, digital platforms have shown both promise and limitation. A study of ride-hailing platforms in Southeast Asia found that while drivers earned more than in alternative informal work, their income remained volatile and unpredictable. Additionally, the platforms' algorithmic management systems sometimes penalized drivers for factors beyond their control, such as traffic conditions.
>
> To summarize, digital platforms are neither the savior of economic opportunity nor its destroyer. They represent a tool that can amplify existing inequalities or reduce them, depending on how they are designed and regulated."

### Lecture 2: Coral Reef Ecosystems and Climate Change
**Audio file:** `audio/listening-lecture-2.wav`

> "Today, let's turn our attention to coral reef ecosystems, which are often called the rainforests of the sea. Despite covering less than one percent of the ocean floor, coral reefs support approximately twenty-five percent of all marine species. They provide food, livelihoods, and coastal protection for hundreds of millions of people worldwide.
>
> However, coral reefs are facing an unprecedented crisis due to rising ocean temperatures. When water temperatures exceed normal seasonal maximums by even one or two degrees Celsius, corals expel the symbiotic algae living in their tissues — a process known as bleaching. Without these algae, corals lose their primary food source and turn white. If high temperatures persist, the corals ultimately die.
>
> Mass bleaching events have become increasingly frequent. In the 1980s, significant bleaching occurred roughly once every twenty-five years. Today, the interval has shortened to approximately six years — far too rapid for most reef systems to recover between events. The Great Barrier Reef, for instance, has experienced three mass bleaching events in the last five years alone.
>
> Scientists are exploring intervention strategies, including the development of super-corals — specially bred or genetically modified strains that can withstand higher temperatures. While promising, these approaches raise ecological concerns about unintended consequences. What happens when a genetically modified organism is introduced into a complex ecosystem?
>
> Ultimately, the long-term survival of coral reefs depends on addressing the root cause: the continued buildup of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere. Without meaningful reductions in carbon emissions, no amount of local intervention will be sufficient to save these ecosystems."

**Questions:**
1. (Gist) What is the lecture mainly about?
   - **Key:** Threats to coral reefs and potential solutions.
2. (Detail) What percentage of marine species do coral reefs support?
   - **Key:** Approximately 25%.
3. (Detail) What causes coral bleaching?
   - **Key:** Rising ocean temperatures that cause corals to expel symbiotic algae.
4. (Inference) What can be inferred about the current bleaching interval?
   - **Key:** It is too short for reefs to recover between events.
5. (Purpose) Why does the professor mention super-corals?
   - **Key:** To describe a potential intervention being explored by scientists.
6. (Attitude) What is the professor's attitude toward local interventions?
   - **Key:** They are insufficient without addressing the root cause (carbon emissions).

### Lecture 3: Color Psychology in Marketing
**Audio file:** `audio/listening-lecture-3.wav`

> "Today we're going to look at how color influences consumer behavior — a field known as color psychology. While the effects of color are often subtle, research shows that up to ninety percent of snap judgments about products can be based on color alone.
>
> Different colors tend to evoke different psychological responses. Red, for instance, creates a sense of urgency and is commonly used in clearance sales. It also increases heart rate and appetite, which is why it appears in so many restaurant logos. Blue, in contrast, is associated with trust, security, and professionalism — which explains its dominance in the banking and technology sectors.
>
> However, color associations are not universal. They vary significantly across cultures. In Western countries, white symbolizes purity and is commonly used in weddings. In parts of Asia, white is traditionally associated with mourning and funerals. Similarly, green may represent environmental consciousness in one context but wealth and status in another.
>
> The strategic use of color extends beyond logos to the entire retail environment. Studies have found that warm-colored environments — those with reds, oranges, and yellows — tend to encourage impulse buying, while cool-colored environments with blues and greens encourage more thoughtful, deliberate purchasing decisions.
>
> Critics of color psychology caution against oversimplification. The impact of any given color depends on numerous factors including the specific shade, the product category, the target demographic, and the broader marketing context. A color that works for a children's toy brand may be entirely inappropriate for a luxury automotive brand. Nonetheless, understanding the basic principles of color psychology provides marketers with a powerful tool for influencing consumer perception."

**Questions:**
1. (Gist) What is the main topic of the lecture?
   - **Key:** How color influences consumer behavior.
2. (Detail) What percentage of snap judgments are based on color?
   - **Key:** Up to 90%.
3. (Detail) According to the lecture, what does the color blue typically evoke?
   - **Key:** Trust, security, and professionalism.
4. (Inference) What can be inferred about color associations?
   - **Key:** They are not universal and vary across cultures.
5. (Purpose) Why does the professor mention weddings in different cultures?
   - **Key:** To illustrate that color meanings vary by culture.
6. (Attitude) What is the professor's attitude toward color psychology critics?
   - **Key:** Acknowledges their concerns but believes the principles are still valuable.

### Conversation 1: Office Hours — Research Paper Discussion
**Audio file:** `audio/listening-conv-1.wav`

> **Student:** Professor Chen, thank you for meeting with me. I'm working on my research paper about renewable energy policy, and I'm a bit stuck on the methodology section.
>
> **Professor:** Of course, that's what office hours are for. What specifically is giving you trouble?
>
> **Student:** Well, I want to compare the effectiveness of different policy approaches across countries, but I'm not sure whether to use a case study approach or a quantitative analysis.
>
> **Professor:** That depends on your research question. If you're asking *why* certain policies worked in specific contexts, case studies would give you richer data. But if you're trying to measure *how much* impact different policies had, you'd need quantitative data.
>
> **Student:** I think I'm more interested in understanding why some policies succeeded and others failed.
>
> **Professor:** Then I'd recommend selecting three to four countries with contrasting approaches and doing a comparative case study. Make sure you justify your country selection in the methodology section.
>
> **Student:** That makes sense. Should I include countries from different regions, or focus on similar economies?
>
> **Professor:** Either can work, but your rationale needs to be clear. If you choose similar economies, you're controlling for economic variables. If you choose different ones, you're testing whether the policy works across contexts. Both are valid — just be explicit about your reasoning.
>
> **Student:** Great, thank you. One more question — should I include my policy recommendations in the conclusion or in a separate section?
>
> **Professor:** Usually the conclusion is sufficient, unless your recommendations are extensive enough to warrant their own section. Keep it concise — professors reading your paper will appreciate that.

**Questions:**
1. (Gist) Why does the student visit Professor Chen?
   - **Key:** For help with the methodology section of a research paper.
2. (Detail) What two research approaches does the student consider?
   - **Key:** Case study approach or quantitative analysis.
3. (Detail) What does the professor recommend for understanding why policies succeeded or failed?
   - **Key:** A comparative case study of three to four countries.
4. (Inference) What can be inferred about the professor's teaching style?
   - **Key:** She encourages students to make their own choices with clear justification.
5. (Attitude) What is the professor's attitude toward having a separate recommendations section?
   - **Key:** It is usually unnecessary unless the recommendations are extensive.

### Task 2: Take an Interview (4 questions)
Answer each question. Up to 45 seconds per answer.

**Questions:**
1. "Describe a time when you had to adapt to an unexpected change. What happened and how did you handle it?"
2. "Some people prefer to work independently, while others prefer to work in teams. Which do you prefer and why?"
3. "Explain a concept or skill you've learned recently. How did you go about learning it?"
4. "If you could change one thing about your educational experience, what would it be and why?"

**Teacher:** Read each question. Start the 45-second timer. Student speaks.

**Model Answers (Band 4+):**

**Q1:** "Last semester, our group project partner dropped out two weeks before the deadline. We had to redistribute all the work. I took on the data analysis portion, which I wasn't originally assigned. I spent extra hours at the library learning the software we needed. In the end, we submitted on time and got a good grade. The experience taught me to be flexible and to step up when things don't go as planned."

**Q2:** "I prefer working in teams because I think collaboration leads to better ideas. When I worked on a research project with three classmates, we each brought different strengths. One was great at organizing data, another at writing, and I was good at presenting. The final product was much stronger than anything any of us could have done alone. That said, I think clear roles and deadlines are important to make teamwork effective."

**Q3:** "I recently learned how to use Python for data analysis. I started with online tutorials, then practiced by analyzing real datasets from my economics course. The hardest part was understanding how to clean messy data. I found that the best way to learn was to work on a specific project rather than just following generic tutorials. Now I can automate tasks that used to take me hours."

**Q4:** "I would change the way group presentations are graded. Often, one person does most of the work while everyone gets the same grade. I think professors should use peer evaluations or individual reflections to assess each person's contribution. This would make group work feel more fair and motivate everyone to participate equally."

### Task 2: Write an Email (15 min)
Write a complete email responding to the scenario. 0-5 points.

**Scenario:** You are a university student. Your professor has announced that the final exam will be moved from Thursday to Monday, giving students one less day to prepare. Write an email to your professor expressing your concern and suggesting an alternative solution.

**Teacher:** Display the scenario. Start the 15-minute timer. Student writes 100-150 words.

**Model Answer (Band 4+):**
> Dear Professor Johnson,
>
> I am writing to respectfully express my concern about the exam being moved from Thursday to Monday. While I understand that scheduling changes are sometimes necessary, this shift gives us one less day to prepare for what is one of the most important assessments of the semester.
>
> Many students, including myself, have arranged study groups and review sessions around the original Thursday date. Additionally, some students work part-time on weekends and rely on the extra day between Friday and Thursday to study. Moving the exam to Monday means we would need to compress our preparation into a single weekend.
>
> I would like to suggest an alternative: perhaps the exam could remain on Thursday but start 30 minutes earlier, or we could receive an extension on the next assignment to compensate for the lost preparation time. I believe either solution would address the scheduling concern while still giving students a fair chance to demonstrate their knowledge.
>
> Thank you for considering this request. I look forward to hearing your thoughts.
>
> Best regards,
> [Student Name]

### Task 3: Write for an Academic Discussion (10 min)
Read the professor's question and student posts. Write your own contribution. 0-5 points. Target: 100+ words.

**Discussion Thread:**

**Professor's Question:** "Let's discuss government priorities regarding artificial intelligence. Some argue that governments should prioritize investment in AI research and development to remain competitive globally. Others contend that regulation should be the priority to prevent harm from uncontrolled AI development. What's your perspective? Do you agree more with Sofia or Liam, or do you have a different view?"

**Sofia's Post:** "Research should come first. If governments don't invest in AI research, they'll fall behind economically and militarily. Regulation can come later once we understand the technology better."

**Liam's Post:** "Regulation is urgent. AI is already causing harm — biased algorithms, job displacement, privacy violations. Smart regulation doesn't prevent innovation — it creates trust that actually enables it."

**Teacher:** Display the thread. Start the 10-minute timer. Student writes 100+ words.

**Model Answer (Band 4+):**
> I agree with both Sofia and Liam to some extent, though I lean closer to Liam's perspective. While Sofia is correct that governments must invest in AI research to remain competitive, I believe this creates a false dichotomy. The most effective approach is to invest in both simultaneously.
>
> What is often overlooked is that thoughtful regulation can actually accelerate innovation by creating clear rules and building public trust. Singapore's balanced approach to AI governance has made it a global hub for AI research while maintaining strong protections. Regulation need not stifle discovery — it creates the framework within which responsible research can flourish.
