TOEFL Integrated Speaking/Writing Format

The Integrated task combines Listening + Reading with a Speaking or Writing response. You must synthesize information from multiple sources.

  1. Read (45-50 sec): A short passage presenting a concept, policy, or issue.
  2. Listen (60-90 sec): A lecture or conversation that engages with the reading (often challenging or qualifying it).
  3. Respond (20 min write or 60 sec speak): Summarize the lecture and explain how it relates to the reading.
  4. Key skill: Identify the relationship — does the lecture challenge, support, or complicate the reading?
Scoring Criteria: Accurate source representation, clear organization, explicit connections between sources, academic language.
Note-Taking Strategies

1. T-Chart Method

Draw a T-chart. Left column: key points from the reading. Right column: corresponding points from the lecture. Draw arrows to show agreement / disagreement.

2. Signal Phrase Tracking

Listen for contrast markers: "However," "But," "On the other hand," "The professor argues that..." vs. agreement markers: "Similarly," "The reading points out," "This aligns with..."

3. Abbreviation System

Develop shorthand: R=reading, L=lecture, P=prof, + = agrees, - = disagrees, ~ = complicates. Use arrows (→) and symbols (∵) to save time.

Integrated Writing Task: Gig Economy Regulation

Reading Passage (1 min)

The gig economy has grown rapidly, with platforms like Uber and DoorDash employing millions of workers as independent contractors. Proponents argue that this classification preserves flexibility — workers can set their own hours and work for multiple platforms simultaneously. Critics, however, contend that independent contractor status denies workers basic protections such as minimum wage guarantees, overtime pay, and unemployment insurance. Several states have proposed legislation to reclassify gig workers as employees, while platforms have spent heavily to maintain the current model.

Lecture Summary — Professor's Argument

"The debate over gig worker classification misses a crucial point. Both the 'employee' and 'independent contractor' models are outdated for the modern economy. Instead of forcing workers into one category, we need a third classification — what some scholars call 'dependent contractor.' This category would grant gig workers access to pro-rated benefits like health insurance and paid leave while preserving their flexibility to choose when and how much they work. Countries like Canada and Germany have adopted hybrid models that offer partial protections without full employee status. The data from these countries shows that such hybrid approaches reduce worker poverty by 22% without significantly increasing costs to platforms. The real question isn't whether gig workers should be employees — it's whether we can design a system that gives them the best of both worlds."
