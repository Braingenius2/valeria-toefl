🎯 Predictive Error Targets
Negative Fact Traps (EXCEPT questions): Distinguishing options mentioned in the text from true assertions that are not directly stated as answers to the negative prompt.
Modifiers in Inferences: Watch for scope-changing adverbs (solely, invariably, exclusively). If the text says "often", options with "always" are incorrect distractors.
🎯 The 4 Distractor Types in TOEFL Reading
1. Outside Knowledge Trap

States a real-world fact that is NOT mentioned in the text. Highly dangerous for knowledgeable students!

2. Scope Exaggerator

Replaces qualified words (likely, partially) with absolute words (always, completely, impossible).

3. Opposite Direction

Reverses cause and effect (e.g. states X caused Y when text states Y caused X).

4. False Detail Combo

Combines keywords from Paragraph 1 and Paragraph 3 into a single misleading statement.

📖 Controlled Practice: Solar Radiation Management

Solar Radiation Management (SRM) strategies aim to reduce the amount of solar energy absorbed by Earth's climate system. The most widely discussed method involves injecting sulfur dioxide into the upper atmosphere, mimicking the cooling effect observed after major volcanic eruptions, such as the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo. While computer simulations indicate that SRM could lower global mean temperatures within months, it fails to address ocean acidification caused by elevated carbon dioxide levels. Furthermore, if an SRM intervention were suddenly terminated due to geopolitical instability or technical failure, Earth could experience rapid, severe warming—a phenomenon known as 'termination shock'—giving ecosystems little time to adapt.

(B) It resulted in a temporary reduction in global temperatures.
(C) It was the first human-engineered solar radiation experiment.
(D) It eliminated carbon dioxide from the upper atmosphere.

(A) Inability to stop ocean acidification
(B) Sudden rapid warming if terminated abruptly

Rationale: The text states SRM mimics the cooling effect observed after Mount Pinatubo. Therefore, Pinatubo must have caused cooling/temperature reduction. Option A is outside knowledge (Pinatubo did affect ozone, but the text doesn't mention this). Option C is false — Pinatubo was natural, not human-engineered. Option D is not supported by the text.

Q2 Answer: (C)
Rationale: Solar power grid destruction is not mentioned anywhere in the passage. Option A is directly stated (SRM "fails to address ocean acidification"), B is stated (termination shock), and D is the closest paraphrase of the passage's listed risks — ocean acidification, rapid severe warming, and ecosystems given little time to adapt. Only C has zero support in the text.

The Green Hydrogen Economy

Green hydrogen, produced via the electrolysis of water using renewable electricity, is increasingly recognized as an essential energy carrier for decarbonizing heavy industries such as steelmaking and maritime transport. Unlike blue hydrogen, which relies on natural gas coupled with carbon capture storage, green hydrogen emits zero greenhouse gases during production. However, widespread adoption faces immense logistical hurdles, including high capital costs for electrolyzers and the transport challenges of storing hydrogen at high pressures or cryogenic temperatures.

The fundamental advantage of green hydrogen lies in its versatility as an energy storage medium. When solar and wind generation exceed grid demand, the surplus electricity can power electrolyzers to produce hydrogen, which is then stored for later use. This addresses the intermittency problem that has long plagued renewable energy adoption. Furthermore, hydrogen fuel cells can power heavy-duty vehicles, ships, and potentially aircraft—sectors where direct electrification remains technically impractical due to energy density requirements.

Despite these advantages, the economics of green hydrogen remain challenging. [A]Current production costs range from four to six dollars per kilogram, compared to one to two dollars for grey hydrogen produced from natural gas without carbon capture.[B] Industry analysts project that costs could fall to two dollars per kilogram by 2030 through economies of scale and electrolyzer efficiency improvements.[C] Government subsidies, particularly the European Union's Hydrogen Strategy and the United States' Inflation Reduction Act, are accelerating deployment by reducing the cost gap between green and grey hydrogen.[D]

Critics argue that investing heavily in hydrogen infrastructure represents a strategic misallocation of resources. They contend that direct electrification through heat pumps and battery electric vehicles is more energy-efficient for most applications, and that hydrogen should be reserved only for sectors where no viable electric alternative exists. Nevertheless, proponents maintain that a diversified decarbonization portfolio including hydrogen is essential for achieving net-zero emissions by mid-century.

📖 Read an Academic Passage — Coral Reef Symbiosis

Coral reefs are often called the rainforests of the sea, and for good reason. Though they cover less than one percent of the ocean floor, they support approximately twenty-five percent of all marine species. The foundation of this extraordinary biodiversity lies in a symbiotic relationship between coral polyps and microscopic algae called zooxanthellae. The algae live within coral tissue, photosynthesizing and providing the coral with up to ninety percent of its energy. In return, the coral supplies the algae with carbon dioxide and a protected environment.

This delicate partnership is now under severe threat from rising ocean temperatures. When water temperatures exceed the coral's thermal tolerance by just one to two degrees Celsius, the symbiotic relationship breaks down. The zooxanthellae begin producing toxic reactive oxygen species, prompting the coral to expel them. Without these algae, the coral turns white — a process known as bleaching — and begins to starve. If temperatures normalize within weeks, corals can gradually recover. However, prolonged thermal stress leads to mass mortality, with devastating consequences for marine ecosystems and coastal communities worldwide.

Conservation efforts currently focus on reducing local stressors such as overfishing and pollution, which diminish coral resilience. Marine protected areas have shown success in maintaining reef health by limiting destructive human activities. Researchers are also developing active interventions, including coral gardening — growing heat-resistant fragments in nurseries and transplanting them onto degraded reefs. Some scientists advocate for assisted gene flow, breeding corals from warmer regions with those from cooler areas to enhance thermal tolerance. Despite these efforts, the long-term survival of coral reefs ultimately depends on meaningful global reductions in greenhouse gas emissions.
