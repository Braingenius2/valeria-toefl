Why Valeria scored 3.0 in L10 Reading & how to fix it:

The Outside Knowledge Trap: Picking an option that is a real-world fact but not strictly supported by the text lines.
Golden Rule of TOEFL Inference: The correct option must be a 100% direct logical extension of 1–2 lines. If it requires 2 steps of guessing, it is incorrect!

Digital Archives and Historical Preservation

The shift from physical paper archives to digital repositories has fundamentally altered how historians conduct primary research. Digitization initiatives allow scholars to access rare manuscripts remotely, democratizing research that was previously restricted to funded institutions. However, digital preservation presents unique technical vulnerabilities. Unlike acid-free paper, which can endure for centuries under stable environmental conditions, digital data is susceptible to bit rot, hardware obsolescence, and proprietary file format degradation.

Consequently, modern archivists must engage in continuous migration—the practice of periodically transferring data from legacy systems to contemporary file formats. This ongoing maintenance requires sustained financial commitment from universities and governments. Without proactive digital stewardship, large swathes of twenty-first-century electronic correspondence and administrative records risk becoming completely inaccessible to future generations of researchers.

Q1 (Inference): What can be inferred about digital data preservation compared to paper archives?
(A) Digital preservation requires continuous ongoing financial maintenance, whereas paper archives require less frequent technical migration.
(B) Paper archives are more susceptible to environmental damage than digital data repositories.
(C) Digital repositories have completely replaced physical archives in modern universities.
(D) Both paper and digital archives face identical preservation challenges over time.
Q2 (Vocabulary in Context): The word democratizing in paragraph 1 is closest in meaning to:
(A) Making accessible to a broader population
(B) Converting to a democratic system of governance
(C) Reducing the cost of academic research
(D) Eliminating the need for physical libraries
Q1 Answer: (A)
Rationale: The passage states that digital data is susceptible to bit rot and format degradation, requiring "continuous migration" and "sustained financial commitment." Paper archives under stable conditions "can endure for centuries." This directly supports A. Options B, C, and D are either contradicted by the text or go beyond what is stated.

Q2 Answer: (A)
Rationale: The text says digitization "democratizing research that was previously restricted to funded institutions." This means making it accessible to more people. Option A is the closest meaning. Option B is a political meaning not relevant here. C and D are not supported by the text.

Professor: "This week we are discussing higher education funding. Some argue that governments should make university tuition completely free for all citizens to promote equality. Others believe students should pay for their education because personal investment increases academic commitment. What is your view?"

Elena: "Free tuition is essential. Education is a public good, and financial barriers prevent talented low-income students from reaching their potential."
Mark: "Free tuition places an unsustainable burden on taxpayers. Income-contingent loans or targeted scholarships are much more fiscally responsible."
Word count: 0 words (Goal: 120+)
Model A — Position: Income-contingent loans over free tuition

While I acknowledge Elena's point that free tuition promotes equality, I strongly maintain that an income-contingent loan system is more fiscally sustainable. This is primarily because universal free tuition places an enormous burden on taxpayers, many of whom did not attend university themselves, which directly impacts public budget allocation for healthcare and infrastructure. Critics often argue that loans discourage students from low-income backgrounds, yet this perspective overlooks the fact that income-contingent models only require repayment once graduates earn above a threshold, making them accessible to all. For instance, Australia's HECS-HELP system has successfully increased university access while maintaining fiscal responsibility, with repayment rates exceeding 80%. Therefore, balancing accessibility with financial sustainability is essential for long-term higher education funding.

Model B — Position: Free tuition is a worthwhile investment

I actually see things differently from Mark on this one. The main reason I support free tuition is simple: education is a public good, just like healthcare. When a country invests in educating its population, everyone benefits from higher productivity and long-term economic growth. Some people argue that free tuition lowers quality, but that really depends on how universities are managed, not whether tuition is free. Germany and Norway have had tuition-free systems for years without sacrificing academic standards. So I think the real question isn't whether we can afford free tuition, but whether we can afford not to invest in education.
