🎙️ Shadowing — Paragraph Architecture
A well-structured paragraph in academic writing follows a clear architectural pattern. It opens with a topic sentence that states the central claim or idea of the paragraph. The body sentences provide supporting evidence, examples, or reasoning that develop the topic sentence. Each body sentence connects to the next through transitions that maintain logical flow. The paragraph concludes with a closing sentence that either summarises the key point or links to the next paragraph. This three-part structure — topic, support, close — ensures coherence and helps readers follow complex arguments without confusion.

Targets: architectural pattern · topic sentence · supporting evidence · logical flow · three-part structure · coherence

🏗️ Paragraph Anatomy: T-S-C

Every strong paragraph follows three parts. Apply this to both AD and Email responses.

T — Topic Sentence

States the main claim. Clear stance. 1 sentence. No evidence here — just the position.

Example: "University campuses should extend library hours during examination periods to support student wellbeing."

S — Supporting Details (2–3 sentences)

Evidence, examples, reasoning. Each sentence develops the topic sentence. Use transitions to connect them.

Example: "Furthermore, late-night study sessions are common during finals week, and many students rely on the library's quiet spaces. Moreover, research shows that access to study environments reduces exam-related anxiety."

C — Closing Sentence

Summarises the point or links to the next paragraph. No new evidence. No tautological closers.

Example: "Extending library hours would therefore directly address a practical need that currently goes unmet."

⚠️ TOEFL trap: Body sentences without a clear topic sentence read as a list. Always open with a claim.

🎯 Mini-Drill: Identify the Parts

Read the paragraph. Label each sentence as T, S, or C:

"Mandatory attendance policies benefit university students. First, regular participation helps students acquire knowledge through discussion. Second, attending lectures demonstrates competence and commitment to academic success. Consequently, universities with attendance requirements tend to report higher completion rates."
T: "Mandatory attendance policies benefit university students." — Topic sentence (claim)
S: "First, regular participation helps students acquire knowledge through discussion." — Supporting detail 1
S: "Second, attending lectures demonstrates competence and commitment to academic success." — Supporting detail 2
C: "Consequently, universities with attendance requirements tend to report higher completion rates." — Closing sentence (summary/link)
🔁 Flow Check — Adverb Position

Architecture holds the paragraph together; adverb position keeps each sentence smooth. Where does the bold adverb go? Decide first, then check.

1. The committee presented its recommendations. convincingly
2. Students consult a single source. rarely
3. This claim is convincing. extremely
4. The library extended its hours. fortunately
1. …its recommendations convincingly. End position (also possible: convincingly presented) — never between the verb and its object (✗ presented convincingly its recommendations).
2. Students rarely consult a single source. Frequency adverbs go before the main verb (mid-position).
3. This claim is extremely convincing. Degree adverbs go before the adjective they modify.
4. Fortunately, the library extended its hours. Comment adverbs open the sentence with a comma — same pattern as Consequently / However / Therefore.

⚡ Proofread pass: adverb position is a 10-second scan — check nothing sits between a verb and its object.

✍️ Sprint #1 — Academic Discussion (10 min)
Academic Discussion — TOEFL format: 10:00

Your professor is teaching a course on career preparation. Write a post responding to the professor's question. In your post, you should: express and support your opinion; make a contribution to the discussion in your own words.

Professor: "This week we are discussing career preparation. Should all university students complete a mandatory internship before graduating?"

Nina: "Internships build practical skills and professional contacts that coursework alone cannot provide."
Tom: "Mandatory internships are unfair — unpaid placements exclude students who must work to pay for their studies."

Focus today: Apply T-S-C structure. Topic sentence = clear claim. 2–3 supporting sentences with transitions. Closing = summary or link. Chunk-write. 120–130 words.

Model (~124 words, agrees with Nina, concedes to Tom, T-S-C structure):

While I understand Tom's concern that unpaid internships exclude students from lower-income backgrounds, I agree with Nina that practical experience should be required before graduation. A single semester in a real workplace teaches skills that lectures cannot: meeting deadlines, communicating with colleagues, and solving unstructured problems. For example, a marketing student who runs a real social-media campaign for a small charity learns more about audience research in eight weeks than in a year of theory classes. Moreover, universities can address Tom's fairness worry by funding stipends and offering flexible, credit-bearing placements. Therefore, a funded internship requirement would prepare graduates for employment without punishing those who need to earn. The key is not whether to require experience, but how to make it accessible to everyone.

Checklist: 120–130 words? Topic sentence = clear claim (no evidence)? 2–3 supporting sentences? Closing = summary/link, no new evidence? One classmate referenced? ≥2 transitions? No "Although…but"?
✉️ Sprint #2 — Email Reply (7 min)
Write an Email — TOEFL format: 07:00

You are a student who drives to campus. Write an email to the campus transportation office. In your email, do the following:

1. Explain the difficulty of finding parking on campus.
2. Describe how this affects your daily schedule.
3. Suggest a solution such as a permit system or shuttle service.

Target: 100–120 words (minimum 100). Formal register.

Focus today: Bullet-2-first planning. Each bullet = one mini-paragraph (T-S-C). Target 100–120 words. Reserve last 60s for proofread pass.
0 words — wpm
Model (~106 words, all 3 tasks covered, each bullet = a mini T-S-C):

Dear Transportation Office,

I am writing to describe a problem that affects my daily schedule: parking on campus is extremely limited, and the main lot is usually full by 8:30 a.m.

As a result, I often park a fifteen-minute walk away and arrive at morning seminars late and stressed. Last week I missed the first ten minutes of two lectures while circling the campus looking for a space.

A possible solution would be a simple permit system with a fixed number of spaces per faculty, supported by a shuttle from the overflow lot.

I would be happy to provide details about peak times. Thank you for considering this suggestion.

Best regards,
Valeria

Checklist: All 3 tasks covered? Each bullet = a mini T-S-C paragraph? ≥100 words? Formal register? ≥2 transitions (e.g. As a result / A possible solution)? Proofread pass done?
