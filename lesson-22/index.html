🎙️ Shadowing — Academic Paragraph
Academic writing under time pressure demands a disciplined approach to both structure and speed. Writers who develop pre-planned structural frames and allocate deliberate time per paragraph consistently outperform those who write without a plan. The most effective technique is chunk-writing: producing complete thought units of twenty-five to thirty words without pausing to edit. This sustained momentum allows candidates to reach target word counts while reserving the final minutes for a focused proofreading pass that catches high-impact errors.

Targets: disciplined approach · pre-planned structural frames · deliberate time allocation · chunk-writing · sustained momentum · proofreading pass

L21 link: try to re-use one collocation aloud — e.g., chunk-writing / proofreading pass / substantive responses.

✍️ Sprint #1 — Academic Discussion (10 min)
Academic Discussion — TOEFL format: 10:00

Your professor is teaching a course on higher education. Write a post responding to the professor's question. In your post, you should: express and support your opinion; make a contribution to the discussion in your own words. An effective response will contain at least 100 words.

Professor: "This week we are discussing degree requirements. Should all university students be required to take at least one course outside their major? Why or why not?"

Nina: "Students broaden their thinking and gain skills they would never discover inside their major."
Tom: "Extra courses take time away from the specialization students chose and pay for."

Technique: Chunk-write. Aim for 120–130 words. Do NOT stop to edit mid-sentence. Use ≥2 collocations: conduct research, address issues, demonstrate competence, provide evidence, draw conclusions, acquire knowledge.

Model A (~120 words, agrees with Nina, concedes to Tom, 3 collocations):

While I acknowledge Tom's concern that extra courses take time away from a chosen specialization, I agree with Nina that every student should take at least one class outside their major. This is primarily because students acquire knowledge more deeply when they encounter unfamiliar methods, and this builds flexible thinking they can transfer back to their own field. For example, an engineering student who studies psychology learns how users behave, which helps her address issues in product design more effectively. Critics argue that non-major courses delay progress toward a degree, yet universities can provide evidence that interdisciplinary training improves employment outcomes. Therefore, extending students' intellectual range with a single outside course is a small requirement that offers substantial long-term benefits.

Model B (~115 words, agrees with Tom — your in-class position — concedes to Nina, 2 collocations):

While I understand Nina's point that students gain valuable skills from courses outside their major, I agree with Tom that extra requirements take time away from the specialization students chose and pay for. This is primarily because depth in a field requires sustained focus, and every additional course reduces the hours available for advanced work. For example, an engineering student who must take three unrelated electives has less time to conduct research in a lab or complete a substantial capstone project. Supporters argue that breadth creates flexible thinkers, yet universities can address these issues by offering optional workshops instead of mandatory courses. Therefore, degree requirements should protect students' time for the specialization they are actually here to master.

Checklist: 120–130 words? Position clear? One classmate referenced by name? Concrete example? ≥2 collocations (acquire knowledge / address issues / provide evidence)? Concession clean (no "Although…but")? Known spelling traps checked?
✉️ Sprint #2 — Email Reply (7 min)
Write an Email — TOEFL format: 07:00

You attended a presentation by Dr. Elena Vasquez about renewable energy research at your university. Write an email to Dr. Vasquez. In your email, do the following:

1. Explain what you found interesting in her presentation.
2. Ask whether there are internship openings in her lab.
3. Ask what skills or coursework she values most in potential interns.

Target: 100–120 words (minimum 100). Formal register.

Technique: Bullet-2-first planning. Write the strongest content (bullet 2: internship details) FIRST. Bullet 1 = context. Bullet 3 = offer/close. Target 100–120 words. Run proofread pass in last 60s.

🔍 Proofread Pass Targets

After the timer, spend 60–90 seconds scanning your email for:

✗ Although…but (pick one) · ✗ desure, phychology, usefull, sourses, researches (spelling) · ✗ Tautological closers (In conclusion,Overall,To sum up) · ✗ Missing collocations · ✗ Bullet 2 too thin (no specifics/statistics)
Model (~105 words, all 3 tasks covered, specific details):

Dear Dr. Vasquez,

I am writing to thank you for your presentation on renewable energy research and to ask about opportunities in your lab. I found your comparison of solar storage costs especially interesting.

I would like to know whether your lab will offer internships next semester, because I am looking for practical experience before my final year. I have completed coursework in chemistry and data analysis, where I learned to conduct research with laboratory equipment and analyze experimental results.

Could you tell me which skills you value most in interns? I would be glad to meet during office hours. Thank you for your time.

Best regards,
Valeria

Checklist: All 3 tasks covered (interesting point / internship question / skills question)? Bullet 2 has specifics (chemistry, data analysis, lab equipment)? ≥100 words? Formal register? No spelling traps?
