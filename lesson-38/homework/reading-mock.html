Academic Passage — Urban heat islands

Cities are often several degrees warmer than the countryside around them. This difference, known as the urban heat island effect, has several causes. Dark surfaces such as asphalt and roofs absorb sunlight and release it slowly at night. The colour of a surface matters as much as its material: a white roof reflects much of the sunlight that a dark roof absorbs. Buildings also block the wind that would otherwise carry heat away. In addition, vehicles and air conditioners produce waste heat. The effect is not evenly distributed: a street with mature trees can be noticeably cooler than a bare road a few blocks away. Researchers measure the difference by placing sensors in parks, on roofs, and along busy roads, then comparing the readings over a full day. Measurements taken at noon can therefore differ sharply from those taken at midnight. The results are used by planners who decide where to plant trees, which roofs to paint white, and where to create small parks. These measures are cheaper than rebuilding entire neighbourhoods, but they work best when several are combined. A single tree, for example, will not cool an intersection that is otherwise full of traffic and concrete.

Academic Passage — The function of sleep

For a long time, sleep was treated as a passive state, a period when the body simply stopped working. Research over the past fifty years has changed this view. The brain remains active during sleep, and some of its most important work happens then. During deep sleep, it replays patterns of activity from the day, strengthening the connections that support memory. During another stage, it clears waste products that build up while we are awake. Sleep also affects mood and decision-making: people who are sleep-deprived tend to react more strongly to negative events and to choose immediate rewards over long-term ones. The amount of sleep a person needs varies, but most adults function best with seven to nine hours. Because the effects of lost sleep accumulate, a single long night cannot undo a week of short ones. Researchers therefore describe sleep as part of the learning process, not as time taken away from it.

Academic Passage — The Dust Bowl

In the 1930s, a combination of drought and poor farming practices turned parts of the American Great Plains into what became known as the Dust Bowl. Farmers had ploughed millions of acres of grassland to grow wheat. When the rains failed, the exposed soil dried out and was carried away by wind. Huge dust storms darkened the sky and buried fences and houses. Thousands of families left the region, many heading west to California. The disaster led to changes in farming: farmers were encouraged to plant trees as windbreaks, to leave fields fallow, and to use methods that kept the soil in place. The Dust Bowl is often remembered as a natural disaster, but its causes were partly human. The drought was natural; the vulnerability of the land was created by the way it had been farmed.
