🎙️ Shadowing — Second-Move Fluency
In structured academic interviews, effective respondents extend their initial positions through purposeful elaboration rather than offering brief, isolated statements. By employing specific expansion techniques — providing concrete examples, articulating underlying rationale, or contrasting alternative perspectives — speakers demonstrate both analytical sophistication and genuine engagement with complex topics.

Targets: purposeful elaboration · concrete examples · articulating rationale · contrasting perspectives · analytical sophistication — all signposts for Move 2.

L18 link: try to re-use one collocation aloud while shadowing — e.g., address issues / provide evidence.

🧩 The 3 Second-Move Patterns (Move 2 at 25–45s)

Move 1 (0–25s): Stance + Core Reason in 1–2 sentences. If you stay in Move 1 past second 30 you will run out (L10 failure). Move 2 must start by second 25–30.

A: Counter-Pivot — acknowledge the other side and refute
Signpost: “Of course, opponents argue that… however, …”
B: Societal Implication — broaden to economic / social impact
Signpost: “Looking at the broader picture, this implies that…”
C: Concrete Analogy / Example — personal or observed case
Signpost: “A clear illustration of this can be seen in…”

💡 Second-move language is functional — vary the signpost, don’t memorise one sentence. Aim to use a different pattern each prompt to avoid repetition.

📢 Round 1 — Technology & Education (2 × 45s)
Prompt 1 — Mandatory Attendance: 00:45

“Do you think universities should mandate in-person attendance for lectures?”

Move 1 (0–25s): Stance + reason. Move 2 (25–45s): pick A / B / C.

0:00— auto-stops at 0:45, or tap again to stop early
A Counter-Pivot: “Some argue mandatory attendance limits adult autonomy; however, regular presence helps students acquire knowledge through discussion that recordings cannot provide, which ultimately helps them achieve success.”
B Societal: “More broadly, enforcing attendance helps universities address issues of disengagement and demonstrate competence in supporting student success.”
C Analogy: “For example, in my programming course students who attended in person were able to conduct research with peers and draw conclusions faster than those who watched recordings alone.”
Prompt 2 — AI in Elementary School: 00:45

“Should artificial intelligence tools be integrated into elementary school classrooms?”

0:00
A: “Critics worry AI reduces creativity; yet when guided, it lets children gain experience with research tools while teachers still provide evidence for correct answers.”
B: “On a broader level, early AI literacy helps children establish credibility as digital citizens and achieve success in later studies.”
C: “I saw this in my cousin's school where students used AI to acquire knowledge about space, then presented findings — engagement soared.”
📢 Round 2 — Environment & Society (2 × 45s)
Prompt 3 — Climate Action: 00:45

“Is individual consumer action or government regulation more important for solving climate change?”

0:00
A: “Some claim individual choices are enough, but without regulation companies lack incentive to fulfill requirements for emission cuts.”
B: “Societally, regulation creates market pressure that allows consumers and firms to address issues at scale rather than in isolation.”
C: “A concrete case is my city where a bag tax led shops to provide evidence of waste reduction within months.”
Prompt 4 — Sugar Tax: 00:45

“Should governments impose higher taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages to improve public health?”

0:00
A: “Opponents argue taxes limit freedom; however, they help address issues of childhood obesity and fund health campaigns.”
B: “Broadly, such taxes signal that public health outweighs short-term industry profit, which can establish credibility for health policy.”
C: “In the UK, a levy led manufacturers to reformulate drinks — a clear illustration of how taxes can achieve success without banning products.”

Thank you for your help.

Best regards,
Valeria
